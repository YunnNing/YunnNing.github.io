Solder void detection in LGA components

Semantic segmentation on X-ray images to find voids inside solder joints, where a human inspector sees only faint grey-on-grey contrast.

2026 · Final Year Project PyTorch · SegNeXt · DeepLabV3+ · Streamlit Individual project. Data sponsored by ViTrox Technologies.

Voids trapped in Land Grid Array solder joints weaken the electrical and thermal path, but they're hard to see: X-ray images give low contrast, irregular shapes, and heavy class imbalance, since a void occupies a tiny fraction of the frame.

I modified and compared two segmentation architectures on the same sponsored dataset — SegNeXt, built on cheap convolutional attention, and DeepLabV3+ with online hard example mining to push the loss toward the difficult pixels the imbalance would otherwise drown out.

Result — mean IoU
Modified SegNeXt88.1%
Modified DeepLabV3+ (OHEM)82.5%

Pixel accuracy: 97.0% and 94.9% respectively. Accuracy is the less informative of the two — voids are small, so a model predicting "no void" everywhere still scores well on it.

Deployment. The winning model runs behind a Streamlit application, so an inspector uploads an X-ray and sees the segmentation overlaid rather than reading a metric. Imagery and dataset are covered by a non-disclosure agreement and aren't shown here.

Consumer footwear study for New Balance

A client-shaped engagement: collect primary data, test five hypotheses, deliver product and marketing recommendations the client could act on.

2024 · Data Visualization SAS · Google Forms · survey design Team of 5. I designed the questionnaire the whole study ran on, owned one of five hypotheses, and wrote the closing recommendations.

The brief was a company losing a customer segment it couldn't see clearly — plenty of data, no way to turn it into decisions. I designed the survey instrument every team member's analysis depended on: ordinal scales with consistent bands so responses could be encoded rather than retrofitted, income captured as ranges to avoid non-response on a sensitive question, and a multiple-choice grid so one question covered four product categories without survey fatigue.

Writing the closing section meant working with a mixed evidence base: two of five hypotheses reached significance, three didn't. I structured the recommendations by confidence level rather than treating every finding as equally actionable — firm proposals where the results supported them, clearly labelled directional guidance where they didn't.

What I recommended

My hypothesis tested whether young sandal wearers also wear calf socks, motivated by a styling trend and its potential as a bundling opportunity. The analysis found 89.7% of frequent sandal wearers never wear calf socks. My hypothesis had argued for a bundle; the data argued against it.

How I reported it. I recommended against a mainstream bundle and scoped it to a limited-edition niche instead, and described the association as a non-significant trend rather than overstating it to make the story cleaner.

Malay lexicon streaming pipeline

Scheduled pipeline that scrapes three Malay-language sources and builds a queryable lexicon as a graph, growing on every run.

2024 · Data Engineering Kafka · PySpark · Hadoop · Neo4j · Redis · Selenium · cron Team of 4. I owned the ingestion and streaming layer: two of three scrapers, the cleaning stage, and the Kafka producer and consumer.

Malay NLP resources are thin. The pipeline combines an official dictionary, a proverb corpus, and live news text: articles are published to a Kafka topic, converted to Parquet in HDFS, word-counted through Hadoop MapReduce, enriched with definitions and part-of-speech tags, and loaded into Neo4j as a graph with synonym and antonym edges. Cron drives incremental runs.

I built the dictionary and proverb scrapers, the cleaning stage that made scraped output consistent enough to load without creating duplicate graph nodes, and both sides of the Kafka layer.

The design decision I'd expect to be asked about

Kafka looks wrong for a batch pipeline — until you notice that scheduling is exactly why you need it. Articles published between scheduled runs sit durably in the topic until the next job consumes them. Decoupling producer from consumer is what makes a cron-driven pipeline safe to miss a beat.

What the evaluation showed. Coverage reached 99.75%, but token-matching accuracy was only 38%. The lexicon held the words and couldn't reliably retrieve them — a tokenisation and query problem, not a data gap. Malay is morphologically rich, so exact-match queries against surface forms miss when the stored form carries different affixes.

Sentiment classification on 6.3M Yelp reviews

Testing whether a fine-tuned transformer beats feature-engineered classical ML without any of the preprocessing the classical approach depends on.

2025 · Artificial Intelligence PyTorch · Hugging Face · BERT · scikit-learn Team of 3, one model each. I owned BERT end to end and the shared sampling methodology.

We dropped 3-star reviews as genuinely ambiguous, removed duplicates, and sampled down to a workable subset. Rather than assuming a sampling strategy, we ran a preliminary experiment comparing stratified against random balanced sampling — then split the decision: balanced sampling for training so the model learns both classes, stratified for the test set so evaluation reflects the real class distribution.

My tuning ran three versions, each answering a diagnosed failure rather than sweeping a grid. Version 1 overfit, so version 2 shortened sequences and raised regularisation. That over-corrected into underfitting, so version 3 walked it back selectively — dropout split asymmetrically between encoder and classifier head, gradient accumulation to keep effective batch size while allowing noisier steps, and early stopping switched from loss to F1, because loss can improve while minority-class performance doesn't.

Bus station management system

Business rules pushed into the database as procedures and compound triggers, rather than left to application code.

2025 · Advanced Database Oracle · PL/SQL · SQL*Plus Team of 5. I built the core operational schema and the transactional logic: scheduling, booking, cancellation, and reporting.

Bus operations carry constraints a plain schema can't enforce. A bus can't run two overlapping schedules; a driver can't be double-booked or assigned on an expired licence; cancelling a schedule has to cascade through every ticket and booking attached to it.

The scheduling procedure detects conflicts with deliberately different tolerances — a bus can't be scheduled within two hours of another departure, a driver within four. One constraint is mechanical turnaround, the other is human rest. Every operation writes to an audit table capturing old and new values, the user, and the outcome, including failures: an audit trail that only records successes can't tell you who tried something they shouldn't have.

Repository

The piece I'd walk through on a whiteboard

Cancellation status can't be computed row by row, because one booking can hold tickets across several schedules — the correct status is only knowable once the statement finishes. I used a compound trigger: the row-level phase cancels tickets and collects affected booking IDs into a collection, and the statement-level phase deduplicates and recalculates each booking once. A simple row trigger would either query a mutating table or write the wrong status.

A bug I found reviewing my own code. Cancellation IDs come from a counter that resets each statement, so a second cancellation collides with existing rows — the trigger even creates a sequence and never uses it. It passes a single-run demo and fails in production. That's the class of defect I now look for first.
